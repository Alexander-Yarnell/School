# Electric Potential

> [!info] [[University Physics Vol 2]] › Unit 2 — Electricity and Magnetism › Chapter 7
> ← [[Gauss's Law]] · Next: [[Capacitance]] →

**Electric Potential** describes electric interactions with energy instead of force. Because potential is a scalar, it is often easier to work with than the electric field.

## Key Equations

> [!equation]
> $$U = k\frac{q_1 q_2}{r}$$
> $$V = \frac{U}{q}$$
> $$\Delta V = -\int \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}$$
> $$V = k\frac{q}{r}$$
> $$\vec{E} = -\nabla V$$

---

## Electric Potential Energy
The electric force is **conservative**, so it has a potential energy. For two point charges:

> [!equation]
> $$U = k\frac{q_1 q_2}{r}$$

> [!note] Connection
> This comes from [[Electric Charges and Fields]] (Coulomb's law). By contrast, the electric field induced by a changing magnetic field in [[Electromagnetic Induction]] is **non-conservative**.

## Potential and Voltage
**Electric potential** is potential energy per unit charge, measured in volts:

> [!equation]
> $$V = \frac{U}{q}$$

Potential difference (voltage) is found from the field:

> [!equation]
> $$\Delta V = -\int \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}$$

The **electron-volt** (eV) is a handy unit of energy.

## Calculating Potential
For a point charge:

> [!equation]
> $$V = k\frac{q}{r}$$

For groups of charges, sum the potentials; for continuous distributions, integrate. Since $V$ is a scalar, no vector addition is needed.

## Field from Potential
> [!equation]
> $$\vec{E} = -\nabla V$$

The field points toward decreasing potential.

## Equipotentials
**Equipotential surfaces** have constant $V$ and are always perpendicular to $\vec{E}$. A conductor is a single equipotential, and charge piles up at its sharp points.

> [!note] Connection
> This matches the conductor results in [[Gauss's Law]] ($E = 0$ inside, field perpendicular at the surface).

## Applications
Van de Graaff generator, xerography, inkjet printers, electrostatic precipitators.
