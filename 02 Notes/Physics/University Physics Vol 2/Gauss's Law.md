# Gauss's Law

> [!info] [[University Physics Vol 2]] › Unit 2 — Electricity and Magnetism › Chapter 6
> ← [[Electric Charges and Fields]] · Next: [[Electric Potential]] →

**Gauss's Law** relates the electric field passing through a closed surface to the charge inside it. With symmetry, it makes finding $\vec{E}$ much easier than integrating Coulomb's law.

## Key Equations

> [!equation]
> $$\Phi = \oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A}$$
> $$\oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$
> $$E_{\text{line}} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$$
> $$E_{\text{conductor surface}} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$$

---

## Electric Flux
**Electric flux** measures the field passing through a surface:

> [!equation]
> $$\Phi = \oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A}$$

> [!note] Connection
> Magnetic flux $\Phi_B$, whose change drives [[Electromagnetic Induction]], is the magnetic counterpart of this idea.

## Gauss's Law
The net flux through a closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by $\varepsilon_0$:

> [!equation]
> $$\oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$

> [!note] Connection
> Gauss's law for $\vec{E}$ is one of the four Maxwell's equations in [[Electromagnetic Waves]].

## Using Symmetry
Gauss's law is most useful when the charge has **spherical**, **cylindrical** or **planar** symmetry: a point charge, a charged sphere, an infinite line, an infinite plane. For an infinite line of charge:

> [!equation]
> $$E_{\text{line}} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$$

> [!note] Connection
> The infinite-plane field $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ is also found by direct integration in [[Electric Charges and Fields]]. [[Sources of Magnetic Fields]] uses the same symmetry approach with Ampère's law.

## Conductors in Electrostatic Equilibrium
- $\vec{E} = 0$ inside the conductor.
- Any excess charge sits on the surface.
- The field just outside is perpendicular to the surface, with magnitude

> [!equation]
> $$E_{\text{conductor surface}} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$$

> [!note] Connection
> A conductor in equilibrium is a single equipotential — see [[Electric Potential]].
