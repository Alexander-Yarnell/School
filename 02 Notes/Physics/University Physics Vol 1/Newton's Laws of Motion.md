# Newton's Laws of Motion

> [!info] [[University Physics Vol 1]] › Unit 1 — Mechanics › Chapter 5
> ← [[Motion in Two and Three Dimensions]] · Next: [[Applications of Newton's Laws]] →

**Newton's Laws of Motion** move from describing motion to explaining it: **dynamics** studies how forces change motion.

## Key Equations

> [!equation]
> - **Net external force:** $\displaystyle \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \sum \vec{F} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 + \cdots$
> - **Newton's first law:** $\displaystyle \vec{v} = \text{constant when } \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \vec{0}$
> - **Newton's second law:** $\displaystyle \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \sum \vec{F} = m\vec{a}$
> - **Component form:** $\displaystyle \sum F_x = ma_x,\ \sum F_y = ma_y,\ \sum F_z = ma_z$
> - **Momentum form:** $\displaystyle \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt}$
> - **Weight:** $\displaystyle w = mg$
> - **Newton's third law:** $\displaystyle \vec{F}_{AB} = -\vec{F}_{BA}$
> - **Normal force (horizontal surface):** $\displaystyle N = mg$
> - **Normal force (incline):** $\displaystyle N = mg\cos\theta$
> - **Hooke's law (spring force):** $\displaystyle \vec{F} = -k\vec{x}$
> - **Tension (object at rest):** $\displaystyle T = w = mg$

## Forces
- A **force** is a push or pull — a vector, measured in **newtons (N)**.
- **External forces** come from outside the system; a **free-body diagram** shows all of them.

> [!equation]
> - **Net external force:** $\displaystyle \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \sum \vec{F} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 + \cdots$

## Newton's First Law
- A change in velocity needs a cause — the **law of inertia**. Inertia depends on mass.
- The first law holds in **inertial** reference frames.
- Zero net force means the object is at rest or moving at constant velocity (**equilibrium**).

> [!equation]
> - **Newton's first law:** $\displaystyle \vec{v} = \text{constant when } \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \vec{0}$

## Newton's Second Law
- The net external force is proportional to the acceleration and points the same way.
- Equivalently, net force is the rate of change of momentum.

> [!equation]
> - **Newton's second law:** $\displaystyle \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \sum \vec{F} = m\vec{a}$
> - **Component form:** $\displaystyle \sum F_x = ma_x,\ \sum F_y = ma_y,\ \sum F_z = ma_z$
> - **Momentum form:** $\displaystyle \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt}$

> [!note] Connection
> The momentum form is developed in [[Linear Momentum and Collisions]]; the rotational version ($\sum\tau = I\alpha$) is in [[Fixed-Axis Rotation]].

## Mass and Weight
- **Mass** is the amount of matter; **weight** is the gravitational force on an object.
- Air resistance acts on all falling objects on Earth, so none is truly in free fall.

> [!equation]
> - **Weight:** $\displaystyle w = mg$

> [!note] Connection
> Weight as the gravitational attraction of Earth is covered in [[Gravitation]].

## Newton's Third Law
- Action–reaction pairs are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.
- They don't cancel because they act on **different** systems (e.g. rockets, swimmers, helicopters).
- Choosing the system is a key step in solving problems.

> [!equation]
> - **Newton's third law:** $\displaystyle \vec{F}_{AB} = -\vec{F}_{BA}$

## Common Forces
- The **normal force** is perpendicular to and away from a surface; on a flat, non-accelerating surface it equals the weight.
- On an incline at angle $\theta$, weight splits into components perpendicular and parallel to the surface.
- **Tension** pulls along a stretched connector; it equals weight for an object hanging at rest.
- **Spring force** follows Hooke's law: proportional to displacement, opposite in direction.
- **Fictitious forces** appear only in accelerating (noninertial) frames.

> [!equation]
> - **Normal force (horizontal surface):** $\displaystyle N = mg$
> - **Normal force (incline):** $\displaystyle N = mg\cos\theta$
> - **Hooke's law (spring force):** $\displaystyle \vec{F} = -k\vec{x}$
> - **Tension (object at rest):** $\displaystyle T = w = mg$

> [!note] Connection
> Friction is treated in [[Applications of Newton's Laws]]. The spring force drives simple harmonic motion in [[Oscillations]], and its stored energy is in [[Potential Energy and Conservation of Energy]].

## Drawing Free-Body Diagrams
- Sketch the object, draw every force on it, and resolve forces into x and y components — one diagram per object.
- Use it to apply the first law (equilibrium) or the second law (acceleration).
