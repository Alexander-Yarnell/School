# Current and Resistance

> [!info] [[University Physics Vol 2]] › Unit 2 — Electricity and Magnetism › Chapter 9
> ← [[Capacitance]] · Next: [[Direct-Current Circuits]] →

**Current and Resistance** covers moving charge: what current is, why materials resist it, Ohm's law, and electrical power.

## Key Equations

> [!equation]
> $$I = \frac{dQ}{dt}$$
> $$J = \frac{I}{A} = nqv_d$$
> $$R = \rho\frac{L}{A}$$
> $$\rho = \rho_0(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T)$$
> $$V = IR$$
> $$P = IV = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}$$

---

## Electric Current
> [!equation]
> $$I = \frac{dQ}{dt}$$

Measured in **amperes**. **Conventional current** flows the way positive charge would. Current density:

> [!equation]
> $$J = \frac{I}{A} = nqv_d$$

where $v_d$ is the drift velocity.

> [!note] Connection
> The ampere is defined using the force between parallel currents — see [[Sources of Magnetic Fields]]. The Hall effect in [[Magnetic Forces and Fields]] reveals the sign and density of charge carriers.

## Conduction in Metals
Electrons **drift** slowly (on the order of mm/s) on top of fast random motion. Their collisions with the lattice cause resistance.

## Resistivity
> [!equation]
> $$R = \rho\frac{L}{A}$$

In metals, resistivity rises with temperature:

> [!equation]
> $$\rho = \rho_0(1 + \alpha\,\Delta T)$$

## Ohm's Law
> [!equation]
> $$V = IR$$

This holds for **ohmic** materials; diodes and other devices are **non-ohmic**.

> [!note] Connection
> $V$ is the potential difference from [[Electric Potential]].

## Electrical Power
> [!equation]
> $$P = IV = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}$$

Utilities bill energy in **kilowatt-hours**.

> [!note] Connection
> $I^2R$ losses are why power is transmitted at high voltage using transformers — see [[Alternating-Current Circuits]].

## Superconductors
Below a critical temperature, a superconductor has **zero resistance**. They are used in MRI magnets. More in [[Condensed Matter Physics]].
